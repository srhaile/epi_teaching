4  Statistical Methods for Binary Outcomes

Now that we’ve looked at some typical methods for continuous outcomes, we switch to binary outcomes. These outcome generally answer some yes/no kind of question:

  • Did the participant finish the test?
  • Did the patient have a heart attack?

4.2 Summarizing binary outcomes

For binary outcomes, we have a few options.

4.2.1 risk difference

The risk difference is calculated as outcome proportion in the intervention group minus outcome proportion in the control group.

Code 
0.405 - 0.252
[1] 0.153

risk difference: Smoking mothers are 15% more likely to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

4.2.2 risk ratio

The risk ratio is calculated as outcome proportion in the intervention group divided by outcome proportion in the control group.

Code 
0.405 / 0.252
[1] 1.607143

risk ratio: Smoking mothers are 1.6 times more likely to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

4.2.3 odds ratio

Finally, the odds ratio is calculated as outcome odds in the intervention group divided by outcome odds in the control group. But what is odds? Odds compare the probability that an event will happen to the probability that it will not happen. If the probability of an event is 75%, we say the odds of that event are 75%/25%, equal to “3 to 1” or just 3. For some other probabilities we get:

probabilities and odds
p 1 - p odds odds (alternative)
0.80 0.20 4.000 4 to 1
0.75 0.25 3.000 3 to 1
0.70 0.30 2.333 7 to 3
0.60 0.40 1.500 3 to 2
0.50 0.50 1.000 1 to 1
0.40 0.60 0.667 2 to 3
0.30 0.70 0.429 3 to 7
0.25 0.75 0.333 1 to 3
0.20 0.80 0.250 1 to 4

A probability of 40% (as we have for low birthweight babies of smoking mothers) indicates odds of 0.667. Similarly, a probability of 25% (for low birthweight babies of non-smoking mothers) corresponds to odds of 0.333. The odds ratio is therefore about 2.

Code 
(0.4 / 0.6) / (0.25 / 0.75)
[1] 2

odds ratio: Smoking mothers gave 2 times the odds to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

4.2.4 Calculating the odds ratio

You may have noticed we weren’t very precise about calculating the odds ratio just now. In this section, we’ll show some basic calculations to get the correct odds ratio. The simplest way to summarize these kind of yes/no outcomes by group is the so-called 2x2 table (Bland and Altman (2000)).

Code 
birthweight |> 
  tbl_cross(col = smoke,  row = low,
            percent = "column", digits = 1)
smoke
Total
non-smoker smoker
low


    0 86.0 (74.8%) 44.0 (59.5%) 130.0 (68.8%)
    1 29.0 (25.2%) 30.0 (40.5%) 59.0 (31.2%)
Total 115.0 (100.0%) 74.0 (100.0%) 189.0 (100.0%)

For non-smokers, we see that the 29/115 = 0.252 of non-smoking mothers had low birthweight babies, compared to 30/74 = 0.405 of smoking mothers. To get odds, we calculate

Code 
29 / 86
[1] 0.3372093

for non-smoking mothers, and

Code 
30 / 44
[1] 0.6818182

for smoking mothers. We do not need the column totals to calculate odds.

To get the odds ratio, we then calculate:

Code 
(30 / 44) / (29 / 86)
[1] 2.021944

That’s a lot of division signs! But perhaps you remember from school that instead of dividing by the 2nd fraction, we can multiply by its reciprocal.

\[ \frac{30}{44} \Big/ \frac{29}{86} = \frac{30}{44} \times \frac{86}{29} = \frac{86 \cdot 30}{29 \cdot 44} = 2.02\] To remember this way of getting the odds ratio, you multiply the numbers on the diagonal, 86 * 30, and then divide by those not on the diagonal, 44 * 29.

4.3 Comparing binary outcomes

To compare these rates with a hypothesis test, we could use a chi-square test, chisq.test(). In order to run this test in R, we first need to set up the 2x2 table. Additionally, since chisq.test() only reports a p-value, we need to find another way to get the odds ratio, which we can do with the epitools package.

library(epitools)
tab <- with(birthweight, table(low, smoke))

## Summarizing binary outcomes

For binary outcomes, we have a few options.

### risk difference

The **risk difference** is calculated as `outcome proportion in the intervention group` minus `outcome proportion in the control group`.

```{r riskdifference, echo = TRUE}
0.405 - 0.252
```

**risk difference**: Smoking mothers are 15% more likely to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

### risk ratio

The **risk ratio** is calculated as `outcome proportion in the intervention group` divided by `outcome proportion in the control group`.

```{r riskratio, echo = TRUE}
0.405 / 0.252
```

**risk ratio**: Smoking mothers are 1.6 times more likely to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

### odds ratio

Finally, the **odds ratio** is calculated as `outcome odds in the intervention group` divided by `outcome odds in the control group`. But what is [*odds*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odds)? Odds compare the probability that an event will happen to the probability that it will not happen. If the probability of an event is 75%, we say the odds of that event are 75%/25%, equal to "3 to 1" or just 3. For some other probabilities we get:

```{r odds}
#| echo: false
tibble(p = rev(c(0.2, 0.25, 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.75, 0.8))) |> 
  mutate(`1 - p` = 1 - p,
         odds = p / (1 - p),
         odds = round(odds, 3)) |> 
  mutate(`odds (alternative)` = c("4 to 1", "3 to 1",

```

A probability of 40% (as we have for low birthweight babies of smoking mothers) indicates odds of 0.667. Similarly, a probability of 25% (for low birthweight babies of non-smoking mothers) corresponds to odds of 0.333. The odds ratio is therefore about 2.

```{r}
(0.4 / 0.6) / (0.25 / 0.75)
```

**odds ratio**: Smoking mothers gave 2 times the odds to have a low birthweight baby than non-smoking mothers.

### Calculating the odds ratio

You may have noticed we weren't very precise about calculating the odds ratio just now. In this section, we'll show some basic calculations to get the correct odds ratio. The simplest way to summarize these kind of yes/no outcomes by group is the so-called 2x2 table (@oddsratio).

```{r twoway}
birthweight |> 
  tbl_cross(col = smoke,  row = low,
            percent = "column", digits = 1)
```

For non-smokers, we see that the `29/115 = 0.252` of non-smoking mothers had low birthweight babies, compared to `30/74 = 0.405` of smoking mothers. To get odds, we calculate

```{r odds_nonsmoker, echo = TRUE}
29 / 86
```

for non-smoking mothers, and

```{r odds_smoker, echo = TRUE}
30 / 44
```

for smoking mothers. We do not need the column totals to calculate odds.

To get the odds ratio, we then calculate:

```{r odds2x2}
(30 / 44) / (29 / 86)
```

That's a lot of division signs! But perhaps you remember from school that instead of dividing by the 2nd fraction, [we can multiply by its reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction#Division).

$$ \frac{30}{44} \Big/ \frac{29}{86}   = \frac{30}{44} \times \frac{86}{29} = \frac{86 \cdot 30}{29 \cdot 44} = 2.02$$ To remember this way of getting the odds ratio, you multiply the numbers on the diagonal, `86 * 30`, and then divide by those not on the diagonal, `44 * 29`.

## Comparing binary outcomes

To compare these rates with a hypothesis test, we could use a chi-square test, `chisq.test()`. In order to run this test in R, we first need to set up the 2x2 table. Additionally, since `chisq.test()` only reports a p-value, we need to find another way to get the odds ratio, which we can do with the `epitools` package.

```{r chisq, echo = TRUE}
library(epitools)
tab <- with(birthweight, table(low, smoke))
tab
chisq.test(tab)
oddsratio(tab)
```

There are small differences between the output of `chisq.test()` and `oddsratio()` here, but that's because one uses a "continuity correction" by default and the other does not.

```{r chisq2, echo = TRUE}
oddsratio(tab, correction = TRUE)
```

This approach is admittedly a bit tedious. We could also use logistic regression, which can be done with the `glm()` function, and the option `family = binomial`. Also, when we look at the regression coefficients, we need to exponentiate them, $e^{\widehat{\beta}}$, to get odds ratios.

```{r logistic, echo = TRUE}
mod <- glm(low ~ smoke, data = birthweight, 
           family = binomial)
tbl_regression(mod, conf.int = TRUE, exponentiate = TRUE)
```

Logistic regression also allows us to adjust for other factors, like mother's age or race.

```{r logistic-age, echo = TRUE}
mod <- glm(low ~ smoke + age + race, data = birthweight, family = binomial)
tbl_regression(mod, conf.int = TRUE, exponentiate = TRUE)
```
